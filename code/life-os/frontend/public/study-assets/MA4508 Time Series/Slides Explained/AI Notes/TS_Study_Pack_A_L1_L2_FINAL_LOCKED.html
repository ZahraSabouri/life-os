Lecture 1Core ideas

Lecture 1 - Core Ideas in Plain English

A time series is ordered in time
Lecture 1 defines a time series as data gathered sequentially in time. The order is not decoration: the value today may be related to yesterday, last month or last year.
Why it differs from ordinary data
In many ordinary datasets, rows are treated as exchangeable. In a time series, swapping the order can destroy the meaning because the pattern is in the sequence.
Observed series = signal + noise
The lecture gives the model idea “observed series = signal + noise”. The signal is the meaningful underlying part we want to estimate; noise is the random/high-frequency disturbance that hides it.
Smoothing is the first tool
Smoothing tries to reduce noise so that the signal becomes easier to see. It does not automatically forecast the future; it prepares the series for interpretation or later modelling.
Decomposition splits the series into parts
Lecture 1 presents components: trend, seasonal, cyclical and irregular. Lecture 2 starts with a simpler “signal + noise” split and says smoothing supports decomposition.
Forecasting needs a model
Lecture 1 describes forecasting as continuation/prediction of the time series using an estimated model, so forecasting is not just drawing a smooth line forward by eye.
Source: ma3508_lecture1.pdf, p.2, p.8-p.12, p.19; ma3508_lecture2.pdf, p.2

Signal + noise mental model

Observed series idea
observed series = signal + noise
Meaning: what you see is a mixture of an underlying pattern and random disturbance.
Use when: explaining why smoothing is useful.
Trap: do not assume the signal is known; the goal is usually to estimate it.
What we usually want
estimate the signal and forecast it
Meaning: reduce noise, identify the meaningful structure, then use an estimated model for prediction.
Use when: connecting L1 overview to L2 smoothing.
Trap: a smoother historical curve is not automatically a forecast model.

Lecture 1Definitions and exam wording

Lecture 1 - Definitions and Exam Wording

Time series
Data gathered sequentially in time are called a time series.
Smoothing
A time-series task/method used to suppress noise and make the underlying signal or pattern easier to see.
Decomposition
Breaking a time series into components. Lecture 1 lists trend, seasonal, cyclical and irregular components; Lecture 2 describes decomposition as splitting a series into signal and noise components.
Change-point detection
A time-series analysis goal named in the overview: looking for points where the behaviour of the series changes. The lecture names it but does not develop formulas in L1-L2.
Model fitting
Identifying a suitable model, such as AR, MA, ARMA or ARIMA, that describes a given time series and estimating its parameters.
Forecasting
Continuation or prediction of the time series using the estimated model.
Signal
The underlying meaningful component that we usually want to estimate and forecast in the “observed series = signal + noise” model.
Noise / irregular component
The high-frequency, erratic or residual fluctuation that can hide the signal. Lecture 1 calls one component “irregular”; Lecture 2 calls the high-frequency part “noise”.

Lecture 2Core ideas

Lecture 2 - Core Ideas in Plain English

Smoothing estimates signal
The lecture starts from “signal + noise”. Smoothing tries to suppress the noise so the underlying signal is easier to estimate and see.
Low-frequency vs high-frequency
Lecture 2 says smoothing supports decomposition into a signal, a low-frequency component, and noise, a high-frequency component.
Small window
A small moving-average window follows the data closely. That can be useful, but it retains more noise.
Large window
A large moving-average window gives a smoother line. But it may lag behind rapid changes and blur local behaviour.
Centred windows reduce lag
A centred moving average uses values before and after the current point, so it avoids the backward-looking lag of an ordinary SMA.
Holt-Winters adds structure
Holt-Winters extends exponential smoothing to capture trend and seasonality using level, trend and seasonal components.
Exam wording: “Smoothing is used to suppress the high-frequency noise part of a time series so that the lower-frequency signal is easier to estimate or inspect.”
Source: ma3508_lecture2.pdf, p.2-p.3, p.6, p.9-p.13
Lecture 2Definitions

Lecture 2 - Definitions and Exam Wording

Moving average
A smoothing method that replaces a value by an average of nearby observations.
Simple Moving Average (SMA)
For window size m, the smoothed value S_k is the average of the last m observations x_k, x_{k-1}, ..., x_{k-m+1}.
Centred Moving Average (CMA)
For window size 2m+1, the smoothed value C_k averages observations from both sides of k: x_{k-m}, ..., x_k, ..., x_{k+m}.
Weighted Moving Average (WMA)
A moving average where nearby observations are multiplied by non-negative weights and then divided by the sum of the weights.
Simple Exponential Smoothing (SES)
A recursive smoothing method for series with no clear trend or seasonality, giving more weight to recent observations through smoothing parameter alpha.
Holt-Winters
An extension of exponential smoothing designed to capture both trend and seasonality simultaneously.
Trend
The long-term movement or current centre/level direction of the data. In Holt-Winters, the lecture separates level L_t and trend b_t.
Seasonality
Regular periodic fluctuation. In Holt-Winters, s_t is the seasonal component and m is the seasonal period, such as 12 for monthly data.
Residual/noise
The high-frequency or irregular part that smoothing tries to suppress; Lecture 2 calls it noise and Lecture 1 calls an erratic leftover component irregular.
Source: ma3508_lecture1.pdf, p.19; ma3508_lecture2.pdf, p.2-p.13

Lecture 2Formula Cards I

Lecture 2 - Formula Cards: Moving Averages

Simple Moving Average
S_k = (1/m) sum_{i=0}^{m-1} x_{k-i}
Symbols: m is window size; x_k is the observed series; S_k is the smoothed value at k.
Use when: backward-looking smoothing with the last m observations.
Trap: first m-1 values cannot be computed with this backward-looking window, so define them as NA or use another formula.
SMA with m=3
S_k = (1/3)(x_{k-2}+x_{k-1}+x_k)
Symbols: k must be at least 3 for this example.
Use when: quick calculation from slide 4.
Trap: do not average future values; this is backward-looking.
Centred Moving Average
C_k = (1/(2m+1)) sum_{i=-m}^{m} x_{k+i}
Symbols: 2m+1 is the centred window length.
Use when: smoothing with values from both past and future.
Trap: endpoints k=1,...,m and k=n-m+1,...,n cannot be centred in the ordinary way.
CMA with window length 3
C_k = (1/3)(x_{k-1}+x_k+x_{k+1})
Symbols: here 2m+1=3, so m=1.
Use when: slide 7 style numerical calculation.
Trap: k=1 and k=n are unavailable because the window needs one value on each side.
Weighted Moving Average
W_k = [1 / sum_{j=-m}^{m} w_j] sum_{i=-m}^{m} w_i x_{k+i}
Symbols: w_i are non-negative weights; denominator normalises the weighted sum.
Use when: nearby observations should not all have equal importance.
Trap: do not forget to divide by the sum of weights.
Window-size intuition
small m -> closer to original, noisier large m -> smoother, more lag / missed local changes
Symbols: m controls the smoothing strength.
Use when: explaining bias-variance intuition in plain English.
Trap: there is no universally best m without context.
Source: ma3508_lecture2.pdf, p.3-p.8
Lecture 2Formula Cards II

Lecture 2 - Formula Cards: Exponential Smoothing and Holt-Winters

Simple Exponential Smoothing
E_k = alpha x_k + (1-alpha)E_{k-1}, 0 < alpha < 1
Symbols: E_k is the smoothed value; x_k is the observation; alpha is the smoothing parameter.
Use when: series has no clear trend or seasonality.
Trap: alpha close to 1 is not “more smooth”; it reacts quickly and follows data more closely.
Alpha interpretation
alpha close to 1 -> quick reaction alpha close to 0 -> very smooth, slow reaction
Symbols: alpha controls recent-observation weight.
Use when: explaining how SES behaves.
Trap: do not confuse high alpha with heavy averaging of the distant past.
Holt-Winters level equation
L_t = alpha(x_t - s_{t-m}) + (1-alpha)(L_{t-1}+b_{t-1})
Symbols: L_t is level/current centre; s is seasonal component; b is trend; m is seasonal period.
Use when: additive Holt-Winters with seasonal adjustment.
Trap: this is the additive formula shown; do not invent multiplicative equations here.
Holt-Winters trend equation
b_t = beta(L_t - L_{t-1}) + (1-beta)b_{t-1}
Symbols: beta smooths the estimated trend.
Use when: updating the trend part of Holt-Winters.
Trap: b_t is not the observation; it is the trend update.
Holt-Winters seasonal equation
s_t = gamma(x_t - L_t) + (1-gamma)s_{t-m}
Symbols: gamma smooths the seasonal component; m is seasonal period.
Use when: updating seasonality in the additive model.
Trap: for monthly data, the lecture example says m=12.
Additive vs multiplicative
additive -> seasonal variation roughly constant multiplicative -> seasonal variation proportional to level
Symbols: seasonal variation means the size of seasonal swings.
Use when: choosing the model idea from a plot.
Trap: Lecture 2 describes both but shows additive equations.
Source: ma3508_lecture2.pdf, p.9-p.12

Proof skeleton from homework: if alpha = 1 in SES

What is being proved: if alpha=1 in SES, the smoothed series is identical to the original series.
Key steps:
1. Start with the SES equation E_k = alpha x_k + (1-alpha)E_{k-1}.
2. Substitute alpha=1.
3. E_k = 1*x_k + (1-1)E_{k-1}.
4. E_k = x_k + 0 = x_k.
Conclusion: when alpha=1, SES gives full weight to the current observation and zero weight to the previous smoothed value, so the smoothed series equals the original series.

Lecture 2R-code Templates I

Lecture 2 - R-code Template: SMA on Simulated Logistic Trend

# 1. Simulate data
t <- 1:100
trend <- 10 / (1 + exp(-0.15 * (t - 50))) # Logistic curve
noise <- rnorm(100, sd = 0.5) # Gaussian white noise
x <- trend + noise

# 2. Apply Simple Moving Average (n=10)
library(TTR)
ySMA <- SMA(x, n = 10)

# 3. Plot the results
plot(x, type = "l", col = "gray", main = "SMA on Logistic Trend")
lines(trend, col = "blue", lty = 2, lwd = 1) # True trend
lines(ySMA, col = "red", lwd = 2) # Smoothed signal
legend("topleft", legend=c("Observed", "True", "SMA"),
       col=c("gray", "blue", "red"), lty=c(1,2,1))
Line-by-line: t <- 1:100 creates the time index. trend is the underlying logistic signal. rnorm(100, sd=0.5) creates Gaussian white noise. x is observed series = signal + noise. library(TTR) loads the SMA function. SMA(x, n=10) calculates a moving average of window 10. plot draws the observed series; lines overlays the true trend and smoothed signal; legend labels them.
What the output means: the grey line is noisy observations, the blue dashed line is the true signal used in the simulation, and the red line is the estimated smoothed signal from SMA. In real data, the true blue signal is usually unknown.
Common coding mistakes: forgetting library(TTR); expecting the first 9 SMA values to be non-missing for n=10; confusing trend with x; putting constants such as col="red" inside a data formula instead of as plotting arguments.

1) Milk data: SMA and SES

m <- 15 # Simple Moving Average

2) Holt-Winters additive model and forecast

# Fit Holt-Winters additive model
HWmodel <- HoltWinters(milkTS, seasonal = "additive")

# Plot the original data and the fitted values
plot(HWmodel)

# Forecast for the next 12 months
library(forecast)
plot(forecast(HWmodel, h=12))

Exam readinessMistakes and self-test

Common Mistakes and Mini Self-test

Common mistakes

  • Trend vs seasonality: trend is long-term movement; seasonality is regular within-period fluctuation.
  • Seasonality wording: seasonal behaviour does not have to mean literal weather seasons; Lecture 1 says usually within a 12-month period.
  • Smoothing vs forecasting: smoothing makes patterns clearer; forecasting predicts/continues using an estimated model.
  • Window too small: it follows the data closely but keeps more noise.
  • Window too large: it smooths strongly but may lag or hide local changes.
  • SMA endpoint issue: the first m-1 values are not available in the backward-looking SMA formula.
  • CMA endpoint issue: centred averages lose values at both ends because they need past and future observations.
  • Weighted average normalisation: divide by the sum of weights.
  • SES alpha interpretation: alpha near 1 reacts quickly; alpha near 0 reacts slowly and smoothly.
  • Holt-Winters formulas: do not invent multiplicative equations; Lecture 2 shows the additive level/trend/seasonal equations.
  • R as magic: know what the plotted lines mean, not just the command names.
Source: ma3508_lecture1.pdf, p.19; ma3508_lecture2.pdf, p.3-p.13

Mini self-test

  1. Give the lecture definition of a time series.
  2. List the five “what can we do with a time series?” tasks from L1.
  3. Explain “observed series = signal + noise” in plain English.
  4. Name the four components on the L1 components slide.
  5. Write the SMA formula and explain why early values may be NA.
  6. Compute S_4 for x={3,7,2,5,4,8,6} with m=3.
  7. Write the centred moving average formula.
  8. Explain why CMA avoids lag but loses endpoints.
  9. Write the SES formula and explain alpha close to 1 versus alpha close to 0.
  10. State the three Holt-Winters components in Lecture 2.
  11. What does HoltWinters(milkTS, alpha, beta=FALSE, gamma=FALSE) represent in the lecture code?
  12. What does forecast(HWmodel, h=12) ask R to do?
Fast answers: 1) data gathered sequentially in time. 2) smoothing, decomposition, change-point detection, model fitting, forecasting. 3) observed values contain underlying pattern plus noise. 4) trend, seasonal, cyclical, irregular. 5) S_k=(1/m)sum_{i=0}^{m-1}x_{k-i}; first m-1 values lack enough previous observations. 6) S_4=(7+2+5)/3=4.67. 7) C_k=(1/(2m+1))sum_{i=-m}^{m}x_{k+i}. 8) it uses past and future values. 9) E_k=alpha x_k+(1-alpha)E_{k-1}; high alpha reacts fast, low alpha smooths slowly. 10) level L_t, trend b_t, seasonal component s_t. 11) simple exponential smoothing without trend/seasonal updates. 12) forecast 12 future periods.
