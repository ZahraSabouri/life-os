Plain-English explanation

Why estimate?
The ARMA equations contain unknown numbers such as μ, AR coefficients, MA coefficients and the white-noise variance. In an exam, data are usually given, so the job is to replace the unknown theoretical quantities by estimates computed from the sample.
Theoretical vs estimated
R(k), ρ(k), aj and σ² belong to the model. R̂(k), ρ̂(k), âj and σ̂² are computed from the observed data.
Yule-Walker role
For AR(p), the sample correlations are plugged into the Yule-Walker system. Solving that system gives the estimated AR coefficients.
Noise variance role
After estimating AR coefficients, σ̂² measures the remaining white-noise variation after the AR dependence has been accounted for.
Exam trap: Do not use the true ρ(k) or true coefficients when the question says “estimate”. Use the hatted sample quantities.

Lecture 11Formula cards

Lecture 11 - Definitions and Formula Cards

Definitions and exam wording

Estimator
A formula computed from the observed time series and used as an estimate of an unknown theoretical quantity. Lecture 11 uses X̄n, R̂(k), ρ̂(k), â and σ̂².
Yule-Walker estimation
For an AR(p) process, estimate the correlation function from the data, then solve the Yule-Walker system to estimate AR coefficients.
AR parameter estimate
The vector of estimated AR coefficients â=(â1,...,âp)T is obtained from â=Ĉ−1v̂.
MA(1) estimation
For xt−μ=εt+b1εt−1, estimate b1 by solving b̂1/(1+b̂1²)=ρ̂(1) and choosing the solution with |b̂1|<1.
Mean of ARMA with intercept
x_t=c+a_1x_{t-1}+...+a_px_{t-p}+ε_t+... μ = c + a_1μ+...+a_pμ, so μ = c/(1-a_1-...-a_p)
Symbols: c is the constant/intercept; μ=E(x_t); E(ε_t)=0.
Use when: The model has a non-zero constant and you are asked to compute its mean.
Common trap: Do not include MA/noise terms in the mean because E(ε_t)=0.
Sample mean
X̄_n = (1/n) Σ_{j=1}^n x_j
Symbols: n is sample length; x_j are observations.
Use when: First step in fitting AR(p) with mean μ.
Common trap: Do not forget to centre the data before R̂(k).
Sample covariance and correlation
R̂(k)= (1/n) Σ_{j=1}^{n-k}(x_j-X̄_n)(x_{j+k}-X̄_n) ρ̂(k)=R̂(k)/R̂(0)
Symbols: k is lag; denominator n is used as in Lecture 11.
Use when: Estimate autocorrelation for Yule-Walker fitting.
Common trap: Do not divide by n-k; the lecture formula uses 1/n.
Yule-Walker matrix estimator
â=Ĉ^{-1}v̂, v̂=(ρ̂(1),...,ρ̂(p))^T, Ĉ=(ρ̂(i-j))_{i,j=1}^p
Symbols: Ĉ is the sample correlation matrix; v̂ is the lag-correlation vector.
Use when: Estimate AR(p) coefficients.
Common trap: Ĉ is symmetric; use ρ̂(|i-j|) in code because autocorrelation is even.
Noise variance estimator for AR(p)
σ̂² = R̂(0)(1-â_1ρ̂(1)-...-â_pρ̂(p))
Symbols: R̂(0) estimates Var(x_t); ρ̂(k) are sample correlations.
Use when: After estimating AR coefficients.
Common trap: Do not use R̂(k) in the bracket; the lecture formula uses correlations ρ̂(k).
AR(1) estimators
â_1=ρ̂(1), σ̂²=R̂(0)(1-â_1ρ̂(1))
Symbols: Special case p=1.
Use when: Fast AR(1) hand calculation.
Common trap: â1 is the sample correlation at lag 1, not R̂(1).
AR(2) estimators
â_1 = ρ̂(1)(1-ρ̂(2))/(1-ρ̂²(1)) â_2 = (ρ̂(2)-ρ̂²(1))/(1-ρ̂²(1)) σ̂²=R̂(0)(1-â_1ρ̂(1)-â_2ρ̂(2))
Symbols: Use ρ̂(1), ρ̂(2), R̂(0).
Use when: Repeated AR(2) parameter-estimation exam questions.
Common trap: Watch signs and denominator 1−ρ̂²(1).
MA(1) estimators
b̂_1/(1+b̂_1²)=ρ̂(1), choose |b̂_1|<1 σ̂²=R̂(0)/(1+b̂_1²)
Symbols: b̂1 is the MA coefficient; |b̂|<1 gives invertible solution.
Use when: Estimate an invertible MA(1) from R̂(0), R̂(1) or ρ̂(1).
Common trap: The quadratic gives two roots; choose the one with absolute value smaller than 1.

Lecture 11Worked examples

Lecture 11 - Worked Estimation Examples

Example 1: ARMA mean

Question type: Compute mean of xt=0.2+0.4xt−1+0.5xt−2+εt+0.7εt−1.
Steps:
Take expectations: μ=0.2+0.4μ+0.5μ because Eε=0.
So μ(1−0.4−0.5)=0.2.
Therefore μ=0.2/0.1=2.
How to check: if AR coefficients sum close to 1, the denominator is small and the mean can be large. Here the denominator is 0.1, so μ=2 is plausible.
Source: ma3508_lecture11.pdf, p.2

Example 2: AR(1) hand estimation

Question: Data 2,6,3,7,2 from an AR(1). Estimate μ, a1, σ².
Step-by-step:
μ̂=(2+6+3+7+2)/5=4.
Centred values: −2, 2, −1, 3, −2.
R̂(0)=[(−2)²+2²+(−1)²+3²+(−2)²]/5=22/5.
R̂(1)=[(−2)(2)+2(−1)+(−1)(3)+3(−2)]/5=−15/5.
ρ̂(1)=R̂(1)/R̂(0)=−15/22.
â1=ρ̂(1)=−15/22.
σ̂²=R̂(0)(1−â1ρ̂(1))=(22/5)(1−(15/22)²)=2.35.
Trap: the lag-1 product stops at j=4, not j=5, because xj+1 must exist.
Source: ma3508_lecture11.pdf, p.9

Example 3: AR(2) hand estimation

Question: Data 2,4,1,6,2 from an AR(2). Estimate μ, a1, a2, σ².
Core calculation:
μ̂=3; centred values are −1,1,−2,3,−1.
R̂(0)=16/5, R̂(1)=−12/5, so ρ̂(1)=−12/16.
R̂(2)=7/5, so ρ̂(2)=7/16.
Then use the AR(2) formulas:
â1=ρ̂(1)(1−ρ̂(2))/(1−ρ̂²(1))≈−0.96.
â2=(ρ̂(2)−ρ̂²(1))/(1−ρ̂²(1))≈−0.29.
σ̂²=R̂(0)(1−â1ρ̂(1)−â2ρ̂(2))≈1.29.
How to check: both coefficients can be negative; the sign comes from the sample correlations and should not be “corrected” by intuition.
Source: ma3508_lecture11.pdf, p.10

Example 4: MA(1) estimation

Question type: Given X̄n=2.3, R̂(0)=1.2, ρ̂(1)=1/4 for an MA(1), estimate μ,b1,σ².
Steps:
μ̂=2.3.
Solve b̂/(1+b̂²)=1/4 ⇒ b̂²−4b̂+1=0.
Roots are 2+√3≈3.73 and 2−√3≈0.27.
Choose b̂=2−√3 because |b̂|<1.
σ̂²=1.2/(1+(2−√3)²)=1.12.
Trap: choosing the larger root violates invertibility.

Core ideas in plain English

Forecast = predicted future value
A forecast is a predictor for a future xn+h. The integer h is the forecast horizon: h=1 means next observation; h=2 means two steps ahead.
Recursive forecasting
For AR models, once you move beyond known data, you plug earlier forecasts back into the equation.
MSE matters
Two forecasts may have similar values but different uncertainty. Prediction bounds use the forecast MSE and the Gaussian-white-noise assumption when stated.
Covariance matrix logic
The general algorithm uses covariance information R(k) to choose the best linear combination of observed centred values.
Mean version of AR(1): the lecture first proves the zero-mean formula x̂n+h=ahxn. In examples with mean μ, use x̂n+h=μ+ah(xn−μ).

General stationary forecast algorithm
μ̂=(x_1+...+x_n)/n, y_i=x_i-μ̂ Γ=(R(i-j))_{i,j=1}^n b_h=(R(h+n-1),...,R(h+1),R(h))^T γ_h=Γ^{-1}b_h ŷ_{n+h}=γ_h^T Y x̂_{n+h}=μ̂+ŷ_{n+h} σ̂²_{n+h;x}=R(0)-b_h^TΓ^{-1}b_h
Symbols: R(k) is the given covariance; Y=(y_1,...,y_n)^T.
Use when: Forecast a stationary/ARMA process when R(k) is given.
Common trap: Keep the order of bh consistent: it runs from R(h+n−1) down to R(h).
AR(1) h-step forecast, zero mean
x̂_{n+h}=a^h x_n
Symbols: a is AR(1) coefficient; h is horizon.
Use when: Fast AR(1) forecasting when mean is zero.
Common trap: For non-zero mean, forecast deviations from μ, not raw values.
AR(1) h-step forecast with mean
x̂_{n+h}=μ+a^h(x_n-μ)
Symbols: μ is known/estimated mean.
Use when: Repeated shop/company sales forecast questions.
Common trap: Do not use xn−1 for AR(1); only the latest value is needed.
AR(1) MSE and prediction bounds
MSE(x̂_{n+h})=σ²(1-a^{2h})/(1-a²) Bound: x̂_{n+h} ± z_{1-α/2} σ sqrt((1-a^{2h})/(1-a²))
Symbols: σ² is white-noise variance; Gaussian assumption is needed for the bound.
Use when: When the question asks for confidence/prediction interval expression.
Common trap: Use σ, not σ², inside the interval width.
Causal ARMA via MA representation
x_j=Σ_{i=0}^∞ c_i ε_{j-i}, c_0=1 x̂_{n+h}=Σ_{i=0}^∞ c_{i+h} ε_{n-i}
Symbols: ci are MA-representation coefficients.
Use when: Theoretical ARMA forecasting questions.
Common trap: This formula assumes causal ARMA and the MA representation exists.
ARMA forecast MSE via MA coefficients
MSE(x̂_{n+h})=σ² Σ_{i=0}^{h-1} c_i² Bound: x̂_{n+h} ± z_{1-α/2} σ sqrt(Σ_{i=0}^{h-1} c_i²)
Symbols: Only c0,...,ch−1 enter the h-step MSE.
Use when: Prediction bounds for AR/ARMA using MA representation.
Common trap: For h=1, the sum is just c0²=1.
AR(p) recursive forecast
For h=1,...,p, use observed values where available and forecasts for future lags. For h>p, x̂_{n+h}=Σ_{j=1}^p a_j x̂_{n+h-j}
Symbols: aj are AR coefficients.
Use when: Practical AR(2), AR(3), AR(4) forecasting.
Common trap: Do not use unknown future observations; replace them by forecasts.

Core ideas

ARIMA vs ARMA
ARIMA says the differenced series is ARMA. The original series may not itself be ARMA until differencing is applied.
Differencing
The lecture defines x̃t=(1−B)xt, so x̃2=x2−x1, x̃3=x3−x2, and so on.
Forecast recursion still rules
ARIMA(1,1,0) can be rewritten as xt=(1+a)xt−1−a xt−2+εt, then forecast by recursion.
SARIMA adds seasonal structure
Seasonal ARIMA uses ordinary differencing and seasonal differencing, then a seasonal ARMA equation with polynomials in B and Bs.

Lecture 13Definitions and formulas

Lecture 13 - ARIMA/SARIMA Formula Cards

ARIMA(p,d,q) definition
a(B)(1-B)^d x_t = b(B)ε_t
Symbols: p,d,q∈{0,1,2,...}; d is the order of differencing.
Use when: Definition questions or when recognising ARIMA equations.
Common trap: Do not call xt ARMA until the required differencing has been applied.
First difference
x̃_t=(1-B)x_t x̃_2=x_2-x_1, x̃_3=x_3-x_2, ...
Symbols: B is the backward shift operator.
Use when: Explain the “integrated” part of ARIMA.
Common trap: Do not subtract in the wrong order.
ARIMA(0,1,0)
(1-B)x_t=ε_t x_t=x_{t-1}+ε_t
Symbols: This is the random walk case.
Use when: Recognise the special case.
Common trap: It is not the same as stationary AR(1) with |a|<1.
ARIMA(1,1,0)
(1-aB)(1-B)x_t=ε_t x_t=(1+a)x_{t-1}-a x_{t-2}+ε_t
Symbols: a∈(−1,1) in the lecture statement.
Use when: Forecast ARIMA(1,1,0) recursively.
Common trap: Use the rewritten difference equation before forecasting.
ARIMA(1,1,0) forecast recursion
x̂_{n+1}=(1+a)x_n-a x_{n-1} x̂_{n+2}=(1+a)x̂_{n+1}-a x_n x̂_{n+3}=(1+a)x̂_{n+2}-a x̂_{n+1}
Symbols: Future unknown values are replaced by forecasts.
Use when: Gold-price style exam calculation.
Common trap: Do not reuse old observations once the recursion requires a future value.
SARIMA definition
y_t=(1-B)^d(1-B^s)^D x_t a(B)ã(B^s)y_t=b(B)b̃(B^s)ε_t
Symbols: s is the seasonal period; d,D are nonnegative integers.
Use when: Definition/explain notation questions.
Common trap: Do not expand OU/outside models; only use lecture SARIMA notation.
Example SARIMA equation
(1-a_1B)(1-ã_1B^{12})x_t=(1-b_1B)ε_t x_t-a_1x_{t-1}-ã_1x_{t-12}+a_1ã_1x_{t-13}=ε_t-b_1ε_{t-1}
Symbols: Monthly case with period s=12.
Use when: Recognise how seasonal terms create lag 12 and 13 terms.
Common trap: It looks like ARIMA(13,0,1) but has fewer parameters, as stated in the lecture.
Source: ma3508_lecture13.pdf, p.6-p.15

Worked example: gold-price ARIMA(1,1,0)

Question: xt−1.9xt−1+0.9xt−2=εt, observations 560,580,640,770,800. Forecast next 3 days.
Identify: (1−0.9B)(1−B)xt=εt, so a=0.9 and xt=1.9xt−1−0.9xt−2+εt.
x̂6=1.9(800)−0.9(770)=827.
x̂7=1.9(827)−0.9(800)=851.3.
x̂8=1.9(851.3)−0.9(827)=873.17.
How to check: forecasts continue the recent upward movement but use the ARIMA recursion, not a simple average.

Example 4: Causality check inside Lecture 13

Question: Show xt=xt−1+5cxt−2−5cxt−3+εt is not causal for all c.
a(z)=1−z−5cz²+5cz³=(1−z)(1−5cz²).
One root is z=1.
Since this root is not outside the unit circle, the process is not causal.
Trap: “root on the unit circle” is not allowed for causality.

1) Lecture 11 AR(p) parameter estimation code

x=c(1,4,2,7,3,1,5,2,6); p=3;
n=length(x); mu=sum(x)/n; y=x-mu;
covg=rep(0,n); corrg=rep(0,n);
for(i in (1:n))
{
  covg[i]=sum(y[1:(n+1-i)]*y[i:n])/n;
  corrg[i]=covg[i]/covg[1];
}
C=matrix(0,p,p); v=rep(0,p);
for(i in (1:p)) v[i]=corrg[i+1];
for(i in (1:p)) { for(j in (1:p)) C[i,j]=corrg[abs(i-j)+1];}
a=solve(C) %*% v; print(a);
sigmasq=covg[1]*(1-v %*% a);
Line-by-line: x is the observed series; p sets AR order; mu is X̄n; y is centred data; covg stores R̂(k) with index shift; corrg stores ρ̂(k); C and v build the Yule-Walker system; solve(C)%*%v gives â; sigmasq applies the Lecture 11 σ̂² formula.
Coding trap: corrg[i+1] corresponds to lag i because R indexing starts at R̂(0) in covg[1].
Source: ma3508_lecture11.pdf, p.6

2) Lecture 12 AR(2) forecasting loop

x=c(46,45,48,46); n=length(x);
a=c(1.3,-0.7); p=length(a);
mu=47; h=40-n;
x=c(x,rep(0,h));
for(i in (1:h))
  x[n+i]=mu+sum(a*(x[seq(n+i-1,n+i-p,by=-1)]-mu));
plot((1:(n+h)),x,pch=19,main="time series (blue) and forecast")
points((1:n),x[1:n],pch=19,col="blue")
Line-by-line: x starts with observed values; a stores AR coefficients; h is how many future values to append; zeros reserve space for forecasts; the loop computes each future x using the latest available observed/forecast values; the blue points mark the original observations.

3) Mock solution: covariance-based forecast code

x=c(3.2,1.3,4.5,2.7); xpred=rep(0,4); xsig=rep(0,4);
givenCov=function(k) { return(1.6*exp(-0.7*abs(k))); }
n=length(x); mu=mean(x); y=x-mu;
Gamma=matrix(0,n,n);
for(i in (1:n)) for(j in (1:n)) Gamma[i,j]=givenCov(i-j);
h=4
bh=rep(0,n); for(i in (1:n)) bh[i]=givenCov(n+h-i);
a=solve(Gamma) %*% bh;
ynh=y%*%a; xpred[h]=mu+ynh;
xsig[h]=sqrt(givenCov(0)-bh %*% solve(Gamma) %*% bh);
confInterval=xpred[h]+xsig[h]*qnorm(1-0.05/2)*c(-1,1)
Output meaning: xpred[h] is the forecast for the h-step horizon; xsig[h] is the forecast standard error; confInterval gives the 95% interval in this code.

Common Mistakes and Mini Self-test

Common mistakes

Fitting mistake: using true parameters when the question asks for estimates. If the question gives data or R̂ values, use hatted quantities.
Yule-Walker mistake: building Ĉ with raw covariances when the lecture formula uses correlations for â=Ĉ−1v̂.
Noise variance mistake: forgetting the σ̂² formula after estimating â, or using σ instead of σ².
MA(1) mistake: solving the quadratic but choosing the non-invertible root.
Forecasting mistake: mixing one-step and h-step forecasts; h-step AR(1) uses ah, not h·a.
Recursive forecast mistake: using unknown future observations instead of previous forecasts.
ARIMA mistake: forgetting differencing. ARIMA means the differenced series is ARMA.
Prediction-bound mistake: using MSE directly as interval width; bounds need the square root of MSE.
R-code mistake: misunderstanding index shifts: covg[1] is lag 0, corrg[i+1] is lag i.
