Lecture 6 Answers 1-7

Answers / Worked Solutions

A1. Clustering is the process of examining a collection of points and grouping them into clusters according to a distance or similarity measure. It is unsupervised because the data are not labelled in advance; the algorithm tries to discover structure from the points themselves.
A2. The ideal clustering has points in the same cluster close to one another, while points in different clusters are far apart. In exam wording: clustering aims for low within-cluster distance and high between-cluster distance.
A3. In the dog example, the points are height/weight measurements. Without being told the dog breeds, the scatter plot visually separates into groups that correspond to varieties such as Chihuahuas, Dachshunds and Beagles. This shows how clustering can uncover hidden groups without labels.
A4. In two or three dimensions, humans can often see clusters by looking. In high dimensions this visual intuition fails, distances become harder to interpret, and the apparent grouping can depend strongly on the scale and viewpoint.
A5. Some data are not naturally vectors in Euclidean space, for example graph/social-network data, user histories or mixed records. In such spaces, an average point may not exist, so centroid-based methods need replacement ideas such as clustroids.
A6. Hierarchical/agglomerative: start with each point as its own cluster and repeatedly merge close clusters. Point assignment: start with estimated centres/clusters, then assign each point to the cluster it best fits; k-means is the main example.
A7. Hierarchical clustering is bottom-up: it begins with singleton clusters and combines them. Point assignment is closer to top-down/centre-based: choose initial representative points, assign observations to the nearest representative, recompute representatives, and repeat.

Lecture 6 Answers 8-14

Answers / Worked Solutions

A8. Begin with every point in its own cluster. While it is not time to stop, choose the best two clusters to merge, combine them into one cluster, and repeat. The word “best” depends on the chosen inter-cluster distance or compactness rule.
A9. A hierarchical clustering method needs: (1) how clusters are represented, for example centroids or clustroids; (2) how to choose which two clusters to merge; (3) when to stop merging.
A10. Common stopping rules are: stop when a predetermined number of clusters has been reached, or stop when a proposed merge would make the resulting cluster too spread out according to a compactness criterion such as radius or diameter.
A11. In Euclidean space, a cluster can be represented by its centroid, the average of the points in that cluster: For a one-point cluster, the point itself is the centroid.
A12. Centroids can be misleading when the shape of the cluster is not compact around a single centre. For example, two differently shaped groups may have similar centroids even though the actual sets of points are far apart or geometrically different.
A13. Possible inter-cluster distances include centroid distance, minimum pairwise distance average pairwise distance maximum pairwise distance and compactness rules based on radius or diameter.
A14. Minimum-distance merging uses the closest pair of points across two clusters; it can merge clusters that only touch at one nearby pair. Maximum-distance/diameter-style merging is stricter, because if the maximum cross-cluster distance is small then the whole combined group is compact.

Lecture 6 Answers 15-21

Answers / Worked Solutions

A15. The radius is the maximum distance from points in the cluster to the centroid: The diameter is the maximum distance between any two points in the cluster: A hierarchical method may merge the pair whose resulting cluster has the smallest radius or smallest diameter.
A16. A clustroid is an actual point from the cluster chosen to represent that cluster. It is needed in non-Euclidean spaces because there may be no meaningful average point or centroid.
A17. Common clustroid choices are: minimise the sum of distances minimise the maximum distance or minimise the sum of squared distances
A18. Hierarchical clustering can be slow because it begins with many singleton clusters and repeatedly merges them one step at a time. With a very large dataset, many distances or updated cluster distances may need to be computed, especially near the beginning.
A19. k-means assumes the data are in Euclidean space, the number of clusters k is chosen in advance, and clusters can be represented by centroids. It is best matched to vector data with compact centroid-based clusters.
A20. Choose k. Select k initial objects/points as centroids. Assign each data point to the nearest centroid. Recalculate each centroid as the average of the points assigned to it. Repeat assignment and recalculation until the centroids no longer move or the assignment is stable.
A21. The partition condition is It means every point belongs to one of the k clusters, and the clusters are disjoint, so a point is not counted in two clusters at once.

Answers / Worked Solutions

A23. The objective measures within-cluster variation because it adds the squared distances from every point to the centroid of its own cluster. Small E means the points are tightly grouped around their centroids. Large E means the clusters are spread out.
A24. Exact minimisation is computationally difficult because it would require considering an enormous number of possible partitions of the dataset into k clusters. The lecture states that the problem is NP-hard in general Euclidean space even for two clusters, and NP-hard for general k even in 2D.
A25. k-means is greedy because each iteration makes a locally improving move: assign points to the nearest current centroids, then recompute centroids. It tries to reduce the objective step by step, without searching all possible partitions globally.
A26. For fixed centroids, each point contributes the squared distance to its assigned centroid. Assigning the point to the nearest centroid gives the smallest contribution among the available centroids, so the assignment step cannot increase the objective.

Lecture 6 Answers 29-35

Answers / Worked Solutions

A29. No. k-means always terminates, but it does not necessarily find the best possible clustering or the global minimum of the objective function. It may converge to a local optimum depending on the initial centroids.
A30. Initial centroids affect the result because they determine the first assignment of points. Bad initial centroids can send the algorithm into a poor local optimum. In practice, run k-means several times with different initial centroids and keep the clustering with the smallest objective value.
A31. Far-apart initialisation tries to choose centres likely to lie in different clusters. Pick one initial point, then repeatedly choose the point farthest from the already chosen centres. A useful formula is then choose
A32. The second strategy is to cluster a sample of the data first, possibly using hierarchical clustering, until there are k clusters. Then choose one representative point from each sample cluster, for example the point closest to that sample cluster’s centroid, as the initial k-means centroids.
A33. A complete 2023-style answer: k-means is a centroid-based point-assignment clustering algorithm. Choose k and initial centroids, assign each point to the nearest centroid, recompute each centroid as the mean of its assigned cluster, and repeat until stable. Its objective is which should be minimised.
A34. A complete 2024-style answer needs algorithm + objective + convergence. State the k-means steps, then give For convergence: assignment to nearest centroid does not increase E; recomputing centroids gives the mean, which minimises squared error for fixed assignments; therefore E decreases or stays the same. Since there are finitely many partitions, the algorithm terminates, although it may stop at a local rather than global optimum.
A35. A complete 2025-style answer: k-means chooses k clusters, initialises k centroids, alternates nearest-centroid assignment and centroid recomputation, and minimises within-cluster squared error. Initial centroids can be selected by choosing points far apart, by clustering a sample first and taking representatives, or by running the algorithm several times with different initialisations and keeping the result with the smallest objective.
