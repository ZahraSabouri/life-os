Lecture 7 Answers / Worked Solutions 1

Answers / Worked Solutions

A1. A meta-algorithm is an algorithm operating on algorithms. In this lecture, the algorithms are often treated as experts. The purpose is to combine several pieces of advice rather than trusting one single learner. This is useful because different algorithms may capture different aspects of the data.
A2. Selecting only the single best-looking learner can be too narrow. A combination can be better when the learners make different errors. The lecture motivates this by analogy: for an important decision, we usually ask several experts and combine their advice.
A3. Boosting/AdaBoost is presented as the idea of combining weak learners into a stronger classifier. A weak learner may be only slightly better than random, but if many such learners capture different information, their combined prediction can be much stronger. The detailed AdaBoost update formulas are not part of this lecture's examinable detail.
A4. A weak learner is a learner that is not excellent on its own but performs better than random guessing. Many weak learners can be useful if their errors are not identical, because the combined system can exploit the partial information each learner has.
A5. In batch/offline learning, all training data is available before training the model. In online learning, data arrives sequentially, and the learner must make predictions on the fly, receive the true label, suffer loss if wrong, and update.
A6. One online-learning round: an example arrives; the algorithm predicts its label; the true label is revealed; the algorithm incurs loss if wrong; then it updates using this feedback.

Answers / Worked Solutions

A13. If halving makes a mistake, then the majority vote among the current consistent experts was wrong. Therefore at least half of the current consistent experts predicted wrongly. Those wrong experts are removed, so the next consistent set has size at most half the previous one.
A14. Let M be the total number of mistakes. Each mistake halves the current consistent set, so after M mistakes at most n/2^M experts remain. Since one expert is always consistent, at least one remains. Therefore This proves the theorem.
A15. For n=64, since 64 is 2 to the power 6, the maximum number of mistakes is 6.
A16. For n=1000, since 2^9=512 and 2^10=1024, log2(1000) is less than 10 but greater than 9. Since the number of mistakes is an integer, the maximum is 9.
A17. Halving is unrealistic because it assumes at least one expert is perfectly correct forever. In real data all experts may make mistakes. Removing an expert after a single mistake may eventually remove every expert.
A18. Multiplicative weights handles the more realistic case where no expert is assumed perfectly consistent. Instead of deleting wrong experts, it reduces their weights, allowing them to remain but making them less influential.

A19. Simple majority can fail because a majority of experts may be consistently wrong. Treating every expert equally forever ignores evidence that some experts are better than others.
A20. Weighted majority fixes a parameter and initializes On each round, sum the weights of experts predicting each label and choose the label with larger total weight. For each wrong expert, update by Correct experts keep their weight.
A21. Eta controls how strongly wrong experts are penalised. A larger eta penalises wrong experts faster; a smaller eta changes weights more slowly. Lecture 7 fixes eta at most one half.
A22. Simple majority always gives every expert equal weight. Halving keeps only experts that have never been wrong and deletes the rest. Weighted majority keeps all experts but reduces the weights of experts that make mistakes.
A23. The theorem states For every expert i, the algorithm's number of mistakes is controlled by that expert's mistakes plus a penalty depending logarithmically on the number of experts. In particular, it holds for the best expert in hindsight.
A24. When the best expert's mistake count is much larger than the logarithmic penalty, the bound becomes approximately So the deterministic algorithm makes roughly at most twice as many mistakes as the best expert, up to the eta factor.

A30. Exam-style answer: A meta-algorithm combines algorithms/experts. The halving algorithm assumes one expert is always correct; it keeps the experts still consistent with previous labels, predicts by their majority vote, and deletes wrong experts after feedback. Weighted majority removes the perfect-expert assumption by assigning weights to experts, predicting by weighted vote, and multiplying the weights of wrong experts by 1−eta. Thus it learns which experts to trust over time without deleting them completely.

A31. Exam-style proof: start with n experts and assume one is always correct. When halving makes a mistake, at least half of the current experts must have predicted wrongly because the algorithm followed their majority. These wrong experts are removed. Therefore, after M mistakes, at most n/2^M experts remain. Since the consistent expert is never removed, at least one expert remains. Hence Therefore halving makes at most log2(n) mistakes.
