09.05.2026MAT700 Lecture 7 - definitions

A. Meta-Algorithm + Boosting Intuition

Meta-algorithm

A meta-algorithm is an algorithm operating on algorithms. In machine learning, it combines other algorithms or experts rather than relying on one learner. Exam intuition: if an important real-world decision should use several experts, a machine-learning decision can also combine several predictive methods.
Memorise: meta-algorithms are a way of combining other algorithms. Weighted majority, halving, and boosting/AdaBoost are examples of this idea.

AdaBoost / boosting idea

The lecture gives the idea of AdaBoost, not the full update derivation. Several weak learners may each be only slightly better than random guessing. If their mistakes are not all identical, combining them cleverly can produce a stronger classifier. The recording describes this as the “wisdom of crowds”: do not necessarily choose one best algorithm; sometimes combine many imperfect algorithms.
Exam trap: do not write detailed AdaBoost formulas unless they are given in the exam. The covered detail in this lecture is halving and weighted majority.
09.05.2026MAT700 Lecture 7 - online learning

B. Online Learning Setup

Batch/offline vs online learning

Offline / batch learning
All training data is available before the model is built. The model is then used on unseen points.
Online learning
Data arrives in a stream. The learner must predict immediately, then update after feedback.

Online learning proceeds in rounds

At each round: 1. A new example/input arrives. 2. The algorithm uses its current model to predict the label. 3. The true label is revealed. 4. The algorithm incurs loss if the prediction was wrong. 5. The algorithm updates its model/weights using the feedback.
Stock example: each morning experts predict whether a stock goes up or down. The algorithm must decide before the truth is known; after the day ends, it learns whether the decision was correct and updates.

Why online learning fits expert algorithms

Every round gives evidence about which experts are reliable. Halving reacts harshly by deleting wrong experts. Weighted majority reacts more softly by reducing the weights of wrong experts.

C. Halving Algorithm - Setup and Steps

Expert setup

We have a set of n experts. Each expert is a binary classifier/predictor.
Expert functions
Experts map inputs to binary labels.
Consistent expert assumption
At least one expert is always correct.
Important: this assumption is deliberately idealised. The lecturer says it is not realistic, but it gives a clean proof and motivates weighted majority.

Halving algorithm

Maintain the set of experts that have made no mistake so far.
Initial consistent set
Update after truth is revealed
Keep only experts that predicted the true label.
Prediction: take a majority vote among the current consistent experts.
Majority vote
Exam wording: start with all experts; predict by majority vote of surviving experts; after the correct label is known, remove every expert that was wrong.

D. Halving Mistake Bound Proof

The theorem

Halving mistake bound
M = total number of mistakes made by the halving algorithm.

Proof pattern to memorise

Let M be the total number of mistakes made by the algorithm. The algorithm is wrong only when the majority of current consistent experts predicted the wrong label. Therefore, after an algorithm mistake, at least half of the current experts are removed.
After M mistakes
Because one expert is perfectly consistent, at least one expert is never removed.
Use the perfect expert
Exam-ready paragraph: every mistake removes at least half of the remaining experts; since a consistent expert always remains, after M mistakes we must still have at least one expert left. Hence the number of mistakes cannot exceed log base 2 of n.
Common mistakes:
  • Removing experts that disagree with the algorithm. Wrong: remove experts that disagree with the true label.
  • Forgetting the consistent-expert assumption.
  • Writing log without saying it is base 2 for the halving theorem.
09.05.2026MAT700 Lecture 7 - weighted majority

E. Multiplicative Weights + Weighted Majority

Why move beyond halving?

Halving permanently deletes an expert after one mistake. This is too harsh when no expert is perfectly consistent. Multiplicative weights keeps all experts but changes their influence over time.

Prediction from expert advice

The concrete lecture setting is stock movement: each day the price goes up or down. The algorithm predicts up/down using expert advice. A wrong prediction costs 1; a correct prediction costs 0. The stock movements may be arbitrary/adversarial, and the best expert is not known in advance.
Goal: keep the algorithm's cumulative number of mistakes close to the best expert in hindsight.

Why simple majority can fail

A naive approach is to follow the ordinary majority of experts each day. This can fail badly, because the majority of experts may be consistently wrong. Weighted majority corrects this by giving more influence to experts that have performed well so far.

The algorithm does not need to know in advance which expert is best. Its total mistakes are bounded in terms of the mistakes of any expert, especially the best expert. Write: Suppose one of the n experts is consistent. Each time the algorithm makes a mistake, at least half of the surviving experts predicted the wrong label and are removed. 09.05.2026MAT700 Lecture 7 - final review

J. Final Memory Path + Common Mistakes

What to memorise cold

  • Meta-algorithm: algorithm operating on algorithms; combines learners/experts.
  • Online learning: predict in rounds, receive true label, suffer loss, update.
  • Halving: maintain consistent experts, majority vote, remove wrong experts.
  • Halving proof: each algorithm mistake removes at least half; one consistent expert remains; bound is log base 2 of n.
  • Weighted majority: start all weights at 1, predict by weighted vote, multiply wrong experts by the penalty factor.
  • Weighted-majority goal: perform nearly as well as the best expert in hindsight.
  • AdaBoost idea: combine weak learners into a stronger method; full AdaBoost formulas are not developed here.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the consistent-expert assumption in halving.
  • Removing experts before the true label is revealed.
  • Removing experts because they disagreed with the algorithm, rather than because they were wrong.
  • Confusing ordinary majority with weighted majority.
  • Forgetting that weighted majority uses multiplicative, not additive, weight reduction.
  • Using log base 2 for the weighted-majority theorem. Its bound uses natural logarithm.
  • Adding Tutorial 3 PageRank/Naive Bayes into Lecture 7. Those belong to Lectures 5 and 4 respectively.
