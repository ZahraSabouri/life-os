09.05.2026MAT700 Lecture 6 - clustering basics

A. Clustering: Definition, Aim, and Difficulty

Definition

Clustering is the process of examining a collection of points and grouping them into clusters according to some distance measure. The goal is: points in the same cluster should be close to one another; points in different clusters should be far apart. It is an unsupervised learning task: the cluster labels are not given in advance.
Dog example: the lecture’s height/weight diagram shows dogs falling visually into three groups. Without being told the breed labels, the geometry suggests three clusters.

Why clustering is hard

  • Data can be very large.
  • The space can be high-dimensional, so 2D visual intuition fails.
  • The space may not be Euclidean, so averages/centroids may not make sense.
  • Clusters depend on the distance measure and on scale: if you zoom in too much, every point may look like its own cluster; if you zoom out, many groups may merge.
Recording emphasis: humans are good at visually spotting clusters in 2D/3D, but algorithms are needed when data are large, high-dimensional, or not directly visualisable.
Common exam wording: do not define clustering as “classification”. Classification predicts known labels; clustering discovers groups without known labels.

B. Euclidean and Non-Euclidean Data

Euclidean setting

In Euclidean space, points are vectors, so arithmetic operations such as averaging are meaningful. This allows a cluster to be represented by its centroid, the average point of the cluster.
Centroid of cluster C_i
This is why k-means is naturally a Euclidean-space algorithm.

Non-Euclidean setting

In non-Euclidean data, there may be no meaningful average point. Examples from the recording: Netflix user–movie data, Amazon user behaviour records, and social-network/graph data. In these settings, distances or similarities may still exist, but a centroid may not.
Exam memory line: centroid = average vector; clustroid = actual data point selected to represent the cluster.

C. Two Main Clustering Strategies

1. Hierarchical / agglomerative clustering

Start with every point as its own cluster. Repeatedly merge the two closest clusters. Stop when a stopping condition is met, usually when the desired number of clusters has been reached.

2. Point assignment clustering

Begin with estimated cluster representatives. Assign each point to the cluster whose representative it fits best. k-means is the main point-assignment algorithm in Lecture 6.
Hierarchical idea
Bottom-up: begin with many tiny clusters and merge.
Point assignment idea
Top-down/representative-based: start from centres and attach points to nearest centre.
Do not write: “clustering means k-means”. k-means is one clustering algorithm, not the whole topic.

D. Hierarchical Clustering: What You Must Specify

General algorithm

Pseudocode:
Start with each point as its own cluster.
WHILE it is not time to stop:
pick the best two clusters to merge;combine them into one cluster;END.

Three decisions

  • Representation: how do we represent a cluster? Centroid, clustroid, or another summary?
  • Merge rule: how do we define “closest” clusters?
  • Stopping rule: when do we stop merging? For example, when we have k clusters.

Distance between clusters

The original distance is between points. Hierarchical clustering needs an extension to distance between sets/clusters.

09.05.2026MAT700 Lecture 6 - merge criteria

E. Centroid, Radius, Diameter, and Merge Rules

Euclidean centroid rule

If clusters are in Euclidean space, represent each cluster by its centroid. Then merge the two clusters whose centroids are closest.

Other merge criteria

Radius of a cluster
maximum distance from points to the centroid/representative
Diameter of a cluster
maximum pairwise distance inside the cluster
  • Radius rule: merge the pair whose resulting cluster has the smallest radius.
  • Diameter rule: merge the pair whose resulting cluster has the smallest diameter.
  • Minimum-distance rule: can merge groups because of only one close pair, even if most of the groups are far apart.
  • Maximum/diameter rule: is stricter; if it is small, all relevant points are close.
Recording warning: centroid representation may be misleading if the real geometry is not blob-like. Example: two very different shaped clusters may have similar centroids.

F. Clustroids for Non-Euclidean Spaces

Why clustroids?

In non-Euclidean spaces, we cannot necessarily average points. So we choose an existing data point as the representative of the cluster. This representative is called the clustroid.

Common clustroid choices

Minimise sum of distances
Minimise maximum distance
Minimise sum of squared distances

G. k-Means: Setup and Objective Function

When k-means applies

k-means is a centroid-based point-assignment clustering algorithm. It assumes data in Euclidean space. It assumes the number of clusters k is chosen in advance, although k may be investigated by trial and error.

Partition notation

Partition of the data
The data set is split into disjoint clusters C_1,...,C_k.

Objective function

Within-cluster variation / sum of squared errors
c_i is the centroid of cluster C_i. The aim is to make E as small as possible.
Interpretation: the objective is small when points lie close to their own centroid. So the clusters are compact internally.
Hardness: exact optimisation is computationally challenging; the lecture states NP-hardness in general Euclidean space even for k=2, and NP-hardness for general k even in 2D. So k-means uses a greedy practical approach.
09.05.2026MAT700 Lecture 6 - k-means algorithm

H. k-Means Algorithm: Exam Formulation

Algorithm steps

  1. Choose a value of k.
  2. Select k objects as the initial centroids.
  3. Assign each object to the cluster with the nearest centroid.
  4. Recalculate each cluster centroid as the average of the points assigned to that cluster.
  5. Repeat assignment and centroid recalculation until the centroids no longer move, or equivalently until the assignments are stable.

What happens in one iteration?

Assignment step
With centroids fixed, each point goes to its nearest centroid. This cannot increase the objective.
Update step
With assignments fixed, each centroid is replaced by the mean of its cluster. This minimises the within-cluster squared distances for that cluster.
Recording version: start with candidate centres, attach each point to the nearest one, recompute centres of the new groups, then repeat until no non-trivial move is possible.
Exam trap: an answer that only lists steps 1-5 but omits the objective function is incomplete for the past-paper style.
09.05.2026MAT700 Lecture 6 - convergence proof

I. Why k-Means Terminates / Converges

Key statement

k-means always terminates, but it is not guaranteed to find the global best clustering.
Objective does not increase
For every non-trivial iteration, the objective decreases; otherwise the algorithm has stabilised.

Proof idea for the exam

  1. With centroids fixed, assigning a point to its nearest centroid cannot increase its squared distance term.
  2. With clusters fixed, replacing each centroid by the mean minimises the sum of squared distances inside that cluster.
  3. Therefore each full iteration decreases or preserves the objective E.
  4. There are only finitely many possible partitions of a finite data set into k clusters.
  5. So k-means cannot keep making strictly decreasing non-trivial moves forever; it must terminate.
Important wording: this is often called convergence in exam questions, but it means convergence to a stable partition/local optimum, not necessarily convergence to the global minimum of E.
09.05.2026MAT700 Lecture 6 - centroid minimises SSE

J. Why the Centroid is the Right Update

The calculus fact behind k-means

For a fixed cluster with points y_1,...,y_ℓ, the point y that minimises the sum of squared Euclidean distances is the average of the points.
Squared-distance function
Set derivative/gradient to zero
Solution

Use in the convergence proof

After assignment, each cluster is fixed temporarily. Updating the centroid to the mean gives the best centre for that fixed cluster under squared Euclidean distance. So the update step cannot increase the objective.
Do not overclaim: the centroid is optimal for the fixed current cluster, not for all possible clusterings globally.

K. Initial Centroid Selection

Why initial centroids matter

The initial selection of centroids can significantly affect the final clustering. Different starts can lead to different local minima of the objective. A practical fix is to run k-means several times with different initial centroids and choose the result with the smallest objective value.

Two approaches from Lecture 6

MethodMemory explanation
Pick points far apartChoose initial centroids that have a good chance of lying in different clusters.
Cluster a sample firstCluster a sample, possibly hierarchically, into k clusters; pick a representative point from each sample cluster, for example the point closest to the centroid.
Recording emphasis: the lecturer prefers the far-apart initial points approach as faster; hierarchical pre-clustering can be computationally expensive for large data.

Past-paper writing sentence

“Since k-means may converge to a local rather than global optimum, the choice of starting centroids matters. We can choose far-apart initial points or use a preliminary sample clustering, then repeat the algorithm with several initialisations and keep the partition with smallest within-cluster variation.”

L. Exact Past-Paper Answer Shapes to Memorise

2023 Q3(d): formulate k-means + objective

Define k; choose k initial centroids; assign points to nearest centroid; recompute centroids; repeat until stable. State the objective as the within-cluster sum of squared distances from points to their cluster centroid.

2024 Q4(d): add convergence proof

After the algorithm/objective, add: assignment reduces or preserves E; centroid update minimises E for fixed assignments; E decreases over finitely many partitions; hence termination. Add: local optimum, not necessarily global.

2025 Q3(c): add initial-centroid discussion

After the algorithm/objective, add: initial centroids affect the result; use far-apart points, sample/hierarchical pre-clustering, or several random/clever initialisations and choose the clustering with smallest E.

Compact model paragraph

k-means is a centroid-based point-assignment clustering algorithm for Euclidean data. Given k, choose k initial centroids, assign each object to its nearest centroid, recompute each centroid as the mean of its assigned points, and repeat until the centroids no longer move. It tries to minimise the within-cluster variation, the sum of squared distances from each point to the centroid of its cluster. The algorithm terminates because each assignment/update step does not increase the objective and there are finitely many partitions, but it may stop at a local rather than global optimum. Initial centroids therefore matter; good strategies include choosing far-apart points, clustering a sample first, or running the method several times and keeping the lowest-objective result.

M. Common Mistakes and Final Checklist

Common mistakes

  • Calling clustering “supervised classification”.
  • Forgetting that k-means assumes Euclidean/vector data.
  • Forgetting that k must be chosen before running k-means.
  • Writing the k-means steps but not the objective function.
  • Saying k-means always finds the best/global clustering.
  • Confusing centroid with clustroid.
  • Ignoring the effect of initial centroids.
  • Using a centroid in a non-Euclidean setting without justification.
  • For convergence, saying only “it stops when centroids stop moving” without explaining why it must stop.
